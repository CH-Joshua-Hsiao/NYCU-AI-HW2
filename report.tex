\begin{abstract}
This report details the implementation and extensive evaluation of the SimCLR (Simple Framework for Contrastive Learning of Visual Representations) algorithm. We explore the capacity of self-supervised learning to extract highly discriminative, general-purpose visual features without the necessity of human-annotated labels. Through rigorous experimentation on the CIFAR-10 dataset, we demonstrate that contrastive representations can rival fully supervised baselines. Furthermore, via transfer learning experiments on the CIFAR-100 dataset, we reveal that self-supervised representations exhibit superior cross-domain generalizability compared to supervised counterparts. Finally, we present comprehensive ablation studies concerning the contrastive temperature parameter, batch size constraints, and the architectural necessity of the non-linear projection head.
\end{abstract}

\section{Research Question and Motivation}
\subsection{Introduction}
In the contemporary landscape of Artificial Intelligence and Computer Vision, the reliance on massive, human-annotated datasets presents a significant bottleneck for scaling deep learning models. In this project, we investigate the foundational capabilities of Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) through the implementation of SimCLR, a highly influential contrastive learning framework. 

\subsection{Motivation and Objectives}
By training an encoder on augmented views of images without human-annotated labels, we aim to discover if the emerging representation space can yield comparable downstream classification performance to models trained purely via traditional supervised learning. Our primary research questions include:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How close can a self-supervised model approach the theoretical upper bound set by fully supervised learning on a constrained dataset like CIFAR-10?
    \item How does the self-supervised representation behave when transferred to a completely unseen domain with higher semantic complexity (e.g., CIFAR-100)?
    \item What is the mathematical and empirical impact of hyper-parameters unique to contrastive learning, specifically the temperature scalar and the projection head?
\end{enumerate}
Furthermore, we seek to understand the generalizability of these self-supervised representations by exploring transfer learning and conducting extensive ablation studies.

\section{Description of Methods}
The methodology leverages PyTorch to build a SimCLR model applied to the CIFAR-10 dataset. \textit{Note: Generative AI tools (e.g., Gemini) were utilized to assist in structuring the codebase, writing boilerplate evaluation metrics, and reviewing the LaTeX report formatting for optimal academic presentation.}

\subsection{Dataset and Augmentation Composition}
CIFAR-10 consists of $32\times32$ RGB images distributed across 10 macroscopic classes. In SimCLR, the composition of data augmentations is the fundamental driver of representation learning. Using the \texttt{torchvision} library, we apply dual independent augmentations per image to create a positive pair $(x_i, x_j)$. The augmentation pipeline includes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Resized Cropping}: Forces the model to recognize objects from different scales and partial views.
    \item \textbf{Random Horizontal Flipping}: Imparts spatial invariance.
    \item \textbf{Color Jittering \& Grayscale Conversion}: Applied with 80\% and 20\% probabilities, respectively. This is a critical component. If color augmentations are omitted, the neural network can exploit simple color histograms as a "shortcut" to match augmented patches from the same image, bypassing the need to learn true semantic structural features.
\end{itemize}
Applying these transformations yields an effective batch size of $2N$ for contrastive learning, where $N$ is the original batch size.

\subsection{Backbone Network Architecture}
A ResNet-18 architecture, traditionally designed for $224\times224$ ImageNet inputs, was specifically adapted for small-resolution CIFAR images. The initial $7\times7$ convolution was replaced with a $3\times3$ convolution (stride=1, padding=1), and the subsequent maximum pooling layer was altered to an identity layer. These modifications prevent the aggressive downsampling of the $32\times32$ input, preserving vital spatial resolution throughout the residual blocks. The final fully connected classification layer was substituted with a generic global average pooling layer, yielding a highly abstract $512$-dimensional representation vector, denoted as $h$.

\subsection{Non-Linear Projector Head}
We employ a 2-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) consisting of a linear layer, Batch Normalization, a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function, and an output linear layer. This head maps the $512$-dimensional embedding $h$ into a compact $128$-dimensional space $z$. 

\subsection{Contrastive Objective: The NT-Xent Loss Function}
We utilized the Normalized Temperature-scaled Cross Entropy (NT-Xent) metric. This loss function calculates the cosine similarity between the representation of a positive pair, scaled by a temperature parameter ($\tau=0.5$). It then applies a softmax-like formulation to maximize the agreement between the positive pairs while simultaneously repelling the representation from the remaining negative samples in the batch.

\section{Experimental Results}
All models were trained using the Adam Optimizer to ensure robust convergence. The SimCLR baseline was trained for 200 epochs with a learning rate of $3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay of $1\times10^{-6}$, and batch size of 256. Downstream linear probing classifiers and fully supervised models were trained for 100 epochs with a learning rate of $1\times10^{-3}$.

\subsection{Phase 1: Baseline Comparisons and Benchmarking}
To evaluate the quality of the learned representations without backpropagating through the encoder, we froze the trained SimCLR backbone and trained a simple linear classification layer on top of the 512-dimensional output $h$. This evaluation method prevents the backbone from adapting to the downstream task, thereby serving as a pure measurement of feature quality.

This was rigorously compared to a randomly initialized frozen backbone (Lower-Bound), which measures the inherent linear separability of untrained CNN features, and a ResNet-18 trained entirely from scratch with Supervised Learning (Upper-Bound).

\subsection{Phase 2: Comprehensive Ablation Studies}

\textbf{1. The Role of Temperature ($\tau$):} The contrastive temperature $\tau$ heavily scales the cosine similarity distributions before they are passed to the softmax function. We systematically evaluated $\tau \in \{0.1, 0.5, 5.0\}$.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{ablation_temperature.png}
    \caption{Ablation of temperature values evaluating the divergence between NT-Xent Loss and kNN Accuracy.}
\end{figure}
\textit{Are the changes of the two learning curves consistent?} \\
No, they are highly inconsistent, which presents a fascinating dynamic in SSL. When utilizing $\tau=0.1$, the NT-Xent loss drops significantly lower ($\sim 1.12$) compared to the baseline $\tau=0.5$ ($\sim 4.70$). However, the true downstream representation quality, measured by kNN accuracy, for $\tau=0.1$ ($82.39\%$) is actually worse than the baseline ($83.10\%$). This occurs because a very small temperature scales up the similarities, making the softmax distribution artificially sharp. It allows the model to minimize cross-entropy easily by confidently separating a few easy negatives, without actually learning a well-distributed, robust representation space. Conversely, $\tau=5.0$ yielded both terrible loss and kNN accuracy ($73.68\%$) because the distribution became too mathematically uniform. This proves that raw training loss is a highly deceptive metric in Self-Supervised Learning.

\textbf{2. The Impact of Batch Size Variations:} Contrastive learning architectures typically rely heavily on massively scaled batch sizes (often 4096 or larger) to provide a sufficient quantity of negative samples per gradient step. 
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{ablation_batch_size.png}
    \caption{Ablation evaluating the stability of SimCLR under severely constrained batch sizes on CIFAR-10.}
\end{figure}
Interestingly, on CIFAR-10 with our ResNet-18 architecture, the performance remained highly stable across all evaluated batch sizes (32, 64, 128, 256), ranging tightly between 82.63\% and 83.86\% kNN accuracy. This suggests an important corollary: for structurally simpler, lower-resolution datasets like CIFAR-10, the manifold of negative samples is small enough that massive batch sizes are not strictly necessary to learn strong discriminative features.

\textbf{3. Architectural Projector Head Design:} We explored the theoretical necessity of the non-linear projector head $g(\cdot)$.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{ablation_projector.png}
    \caption{Ablation evaluating the classification utility of features before vs. after the non-linear projection head.}
\end{figure}
Training the contrastive loss directly on the 512-dimensional encoder output (No Projector) resulted in a performance drop to 80.94\% (compared to baseline's 82.82\%). Furthermore, evaluating linear probing on the 128-dimensional projector outputs $z$ instead of the 512-dimensional encoder outputs $h$ yielded 78.67\%. This empirically confirms that the non-linear projector head acts as an "augmentation absorber". It absorbs invariant transformations, leaving the preceding encoder representations $h$ much richer in structural details required for general-purpose downstream tasks.

\section{Extensive Discussion and Analysis}

\subsection{Validation of Theoretical Expectations}
\textbf{Are the observed behaviors what you expect?} \\
Yes, the overarching behaviors perfectly mirrored theoretical expectations documented in modern self-supervised literature, alongside several interesting nuances observed during training. First, the SimCLR model successfully learned highly discriminative features without relying on external labels. The learning curves revealed that the K-NN accuracy surged rapidly during the first 50 epochs before gradually plateauing, which is a classic stabilization trajectory for contrastive representation spaces. We also confirmed that continuous kNN monitoring is absolutely vital; the raw NT-Xent loss curve is entirely disconnected from actual representation quality (as definitively proven by our mathematical analysis of the temperature ablation). One slightly unexpected observation was the Batch Size ablation. While literature emphasizes that SimCLR requires massive batch sizes to provide enough negative samples to prevent trivial solutions, our experiments on CIFAR-10 showed remarkable resilience even down to a batch size of 32. This observation suggests that for a low-diversity dataset like CIFAR-10, smaller batches still capture sufficient negative distribution variance to form distinct, linearly separable feature clusters.

\subsection{Domain Gap and Dataset Diversity Constraints}
\textbf{Factors affecting the results and Transferability:} \\
The most profound finding of this research was the Transfer Learning experiment, which distinctly highlighted the impact of the "Domain Gap" and "Dataset Diversity" factors. While Supervised Learning utterly dominated on CIFAR-10 (92.26\%), it severely overfitted to those 10 specific macroscopic semantic classes (e.g., distinguishing a dog from an airplane). When tested on CIFAR-100, the SL backbone struggled significantly (43.38\%) because its objective function had strictly trained it to treat fine-grained details (e.g., the specific shape of a leaf, the texture of an insect, or the breed of a mammal) as irrelevant noise during CIFAR-10 training. In sharp contrast, SimCLR was forced to learn general-purpose, instance-level discriminative features simply to separate heavy image augmentations. Because it learned "what makes an image unique" rather than "what makes this a dog", its representations were much richer and transferred significantly better to CIFAR-100 (45.43\%). 

However, it is critically important to contextualize that 45.43\% is still objectively low for CIFAR-100 classification. This limitation is fundamentally restricted by the characteristics of the pre-training dataset itself. True foundation models (like CLIP or DINO) derive their immense power from massive, highly varied datasets (like ImageNet or LAION). Because CIFAR-10 possesses an extremely narrow scope of visual diversity, it acts as a permanent ceiling for transferability, regardless of how well the contrastive objective is mathematically optimized.

\subsection{Future Trajectories and Remaining Questions}
\textbf{What I Learned and Extrapolations for Future Work:} \\
Through these rigorous experiments, I learned that while Supervised Learning provides the fastest mathematical path to high accuracy on a highly specific target task, it produces inherently brittle feature manifolds compared to SSL when shifted to novel domains. I also learned that while contrastive loss gracefully minimizes the distance between augmented views, evaluating that loss alone in a vacuum is meaningless without continuously measuring downstream utility. 

If more computational time and resources were available, I would systematically test the impact of different individual augmentation types (e.g., performing an ablation where color jittering is completely removed while spatial cropping remains). SimCLR is known to rely heavily on the precise composition of specific augmentations to prevent the network from discovering trivial "shortcuts" (like exploiting chromatic aberration). Furthermore, I would explore the impact of drastically increasing the training duration (e.g., scaling to 1000 or 2000 epochs) to determine if SimCLR's asymptotic performance limit could eventually surpass Supervised performance directly on CIFAR-10. Finally, I would investigate Semi-Supervised Learning regimes—specifically, utilizing the SSL pre-trained backbone and fine-tuning it with only 1\% or 10\% of the available CIFAR-10 labels—to fully demonstrate the label-efficiency promised by foundation models.
